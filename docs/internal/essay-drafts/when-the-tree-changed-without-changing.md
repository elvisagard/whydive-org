# When the Tree Changed Without Changing

Genesis 3:1–7 and the Reality Behind a Competing Picture of God

![Two figures look toward a fruit-bearing tree in a lush garden, with a curved optical distortion suggesting changed perception.](assets/when-the-tree-changed-without-changing-v1.png)

Nothing in the garden has changed when the serpent begins speaking.

The trees are still there. The food God provided is still there. The man and the woman are still living inside a world they did not create and did not have to provide for themselves. The tree in the middle of the garden is still there. The boundary is still there. God’s words have not changed.

What changes first is the picture of that world placed before the woman.

That matters because Genesis 3:1–7 is not merely a story about humans breaking a rule. It is also a story about how a competing representation of God can change the way humans perceive an unchanged reality—and therefore change how they live within it.

The change begins with words.

## 1. Nothing Has Changed Yet

God’s instruction concerning the tree had come inside abundance.

The man could freely eat from the trees of the garden. Within that provision was one boundary: one tree from which he was not to eat, accompanied by a warning about death.

That order matters.

Provision came before prohibition.

The boundary did not create a world of scarcity. It existed inside a world already characterized by provision.

Then the serpent asks whether God had said they could not eat from the trees of the garden.

The question changes the proportions.

God’s instruction had begun with abundance and contained a boundary. The serpent’s question makes the boundary sound as though it describes the abundance.

Yet the garden itself has not changed.

There is no new shortage.

No food has disappeared.

God has not withdrawn anything that had previously been available.

The first competing reality in Genesis 3 is therefore not another physical reality.

It is another representation of the reality God has already provided.

And the creature supplying that representation will become stranger the longer it speaks.

## 2. A Strange Creature Becomes Stranger

Genesis introduces the serpent as one of the creatures of the field God had made.

That matters.

Nothing in the narrative has prepared the reader to encounter another creator, another god, or another independent source of reality.

The serpent belongs to the created world.

But it immediately begins displaying capacities unlike anything Genesis has previously shown us about the other creatures.

We should be careful here.

Genesis does not tell us that the woman is shocked that a serpent speaks. It does not tell us that she considers the encounter anomalous. We should not import our modern experience of serpents into her mind.

The anomaly belongs first to the reader.

We have already watched the man encounter the other creatures. He exercised judgment in relation to them, even naming them. Nothing Genesis has previously shown us about those creatures prepares us for one of them to enter a sophisticated conversation about God.

But the serpent’s strangeness does not stop with speech.

It understands.

It responds.

It reasons.

It knows something about God’s instruction.

Then it contradicts God’s account of the consequence.

Then it speaks as though it knows what God knows.

The anomaly grows with the conversation.

This creature is not merely making sounds.

It increasingly presents itself as an informational rival.

And its attention is remarkably selective.

The garden is full of realities it could discuss.

Life.

Food.

Beauty.

Human relationship.

Work.

Responsibility.

The abundance surrounding the humans.

Yet the serpent’s attention is concentrated on the boundary.

The one prohibition becomes the point around which the conversation turns.

That narrow focus matters because the serpent is not merely displaying intelligence.

Genesis lets us watch how that intelligence is being used.

## 3. The Woman Does Not Begin by Believing It

The woman’s response deserves careful attention.

She corrects the serpent.

“We may eat,” she says.

Those words matter.

The serpent has placed prohibition at the center of its representation of God’s instruction.

The woman restores permission.

She knows that the garden is not generally forbidden to them.

She also knows there is a particular boundary. She identifies the tree and associates crossing that boundary with death.

There are questions we can preserve about the exact wording she uses. She includes “neither shall ye touch it,” although Genesis has not told us where that wording came from. Her wording concerning death is not verbally identical to the wording previously given to the man.

But difference in wording is not automatically distortion.

And those questions should not cause us to overlook what is clearly present.

The woman understands the serpent.

She retrieves relevant information.

She corrects the scope of its representation.

She preserves the general provision.

She knows there is a particular prohibition.

She knows the prohibition carries a serious consequence.

Genesis has not introduced us to a gullible woman.

It has not shown us a woman intellectually overwhelmed because her husband is not participating in the conversation.

It has not shown us a woman already attracted to the prohibited tree.

It has not shown us a woman who distrusts God.

Her first recorded response to a distorted picture of God’s instruction is to correct it.

And she does so in the plural:

“We may eat.”

Whatever the man’s physical location at that moment—and Genesis does not tell us—he remains part of the human situation as she understands it.

The provision is ours.

The boundary is ours.

The consequence concerns us.

That is the woman who enters the rest of the conversation.

## 4. How to Turn Provision Into Deprivation

The serpent does not stop after the woman corrects it.

It intensifies the challenge.

“Ye shall not surely die.”

The opening question had distorted the proportions of God’s instruction.

Now the serpent directly contradicts the consequence God associated with crossing the boundary.

Then it offers an explanation.

God knows something.

Eating will open their eyes.

They will gain knowledge.

Their condition will change.

At first glance, the serpent seems simply to be offering different facts.

But something more sophisticated is happening.

The serpent is taking realities that already exist in God’s disclosure and changing the relationships among them.

God had provided abundance.

The serpent does not deny that the garden exists. Instead, the existence of one boundary now becomes capable of making the abundance look incomplete.

Provision can now be seen as containing deprivation.

God had established a boundary.

The serpent does not need to deny the tree exists or that God prohibited eating from it.

Instead, the boundary becomes connected with something desirable on the other side.

A boundary can now be seen as blocked advancement.

God had warned of death.

The serpent directly denies that consequence.

Then the warning itself becomes susceptible to another interpretation.

If death is not the real outcome, why did God say it?

The warning can now be heard not as consequential information but as something standing between the humans and gain.

God knows something the humans do not.

That difference in knowledge was already present. Creatures do not have to know everything God knows in order to know enough to live responsibly.

But the serpent rearranges that relationship too.

The difference between divine knowledge and human knowledge can now suggest that God possesses an advantage the humans are being prevented from acquiring.

Knowledge asymmetry can now be seen as concealed benefit.

Notice what has happened.

The serpent has not had to invent a new tree.

It has not had to invent the boundary.

It has not had to invent God’s greater knowledge.

It has not even had to invent “knowledge of good and evil.” God had already identified the tree in those terms.

The serpent takes realities already disclosed and reconnects them.

Provision becomes deprivation.

Boundary becomes obstruction.

Warning becomes falsehood.

Difference in knowledge becomes suspicious concealment.

Present human condition becomes something that can be improved.

Boundary crossing becomes gain.

This is the brilliance of the distortion.

The serpent can remain close to the existing pieces while radically changing what those pieces mean in relation to one another.

It does not have to create another reality.

It can use God’s disclosed reality as the raw material for constructing another interpretation of that reality.

## 5. Nothing Comparable Has Been Provided

And here the imbalance between God and the serpent becomes enormous.

The humans are not standing between two equally unsupported pictures of reality.

They are already living inside what God has provided.

The food is not a promise waiting to be fulfilled.

They can eat it.

The garden is not an argument.

They inhabit it.

Their capacities are not predictions about what they might someday possess.

They are already using them.

Human relationship is not a prospective benefit.

It is already part of their experience.

Even the freedom the serpent’s opening question places under suspicion is visible in the woman’s own correction:

“We may eat.”

God has supplied both provision and an accumulated evidentiary history.

The humans have experienced what God has done.

The reader has repeatedly watched God speak and reality correspond.

The boundary itself came after provision, not before it.

The serpent supplies nothing comparable.

No alternative garden.

No alternative food.

No demonstrated source of life.

No new human capacity.

No competing history of beneficence.

But the asymmetry is not merely material.

The serpent also supplies no evidence that would make its propositions more reasonable than the account it is challenging.

It does not demonstrate that God has lied.

It gives no counterexample to God’s warning.

It does not show them someone who crossed the boundary and prospered.

It offers no previous statement by which its own reliability can be tested.

It does not explain how it knows what God knows.

It provides no evidence that the knowledge it associates with eating will improve the human condition.

The serpent does not defeat God’s evidence with better evidence.

It supplies ideas about the meaning of evidence God has already supplied.

In fact, the serpent has to build its entire alternative picture out of things God has already provided.

The tree belongs to God’s created reality.

The humans’ capacity for knowledge belongs to God’s created reality.

Their intelligence is already theirs.

Their agency is already theirs.

Even the possibility the serpent places before them depends upon a world the serpent did not make.

So trusting God’s warning at this point would not require evidence-free belief.

The humans already have a history to remember and a reality to examine.

Nothing the serpent has provided gives them an evidentiary reason to trust its picture of God over the God whose provision they are already living inside.

And yet it works.

Not because the serpent has supplied better evidence.

It has not.

Not because it has supplied a better world.

It has supplied no world at all.

Its representation nevertheless becomes influential.

That may be one of the most sobering discoveries in the passage:

an idea does not have to earn the right to be believed before it can change the way we see.

## 6. The Tree Becomes Desirable

Then Genesis 3:6 gives us something new.

The woman sees that the tree is good for food.

She sees that it is pleasant to the eyes.

She sees it as desirable in relation to the knowledge or wisdom-associated gain that has just been placed before her.

This is the first point where Genesis actually shows the prohibited alternative becoming attractive to her.

That timing matters.

The tree was already there.

She had seen it before.

It was accessible before.

The possibility of eating from it existed before.

Even after the serpent presented possible benefits, Genesis had not yet told us that the woman found those benefits attractive.

Now it does.

And this is what makes the moment so striking:

the tree has not changed.

Nothing says its fruit has suddenly become more nutritious.

Nothing says its appearance has changed.

Nothing says God has altered it.

Nothing says the garden has changed around it.

The disclosed development between the woman’s earlier boundary-preserving response and this new positive evaluation is the conversation.

Previously, the tree belonged in the relationship:

tree → do not eat.

Now the woman sees:

tree → good for food.

The prohibited use has entered her positive evaluation.

She also sees:

tree → desirable → knowledge/wisdom gain.

The serpent does not simply get her to repeat something it said.

Something more consequential has happened.

The serpent had taken God’s realities and rearranged their relationships.

Now the woman begins evaluating God’s reality inside those rearranged relationships herself.

The serpent does not say:

“The tree is good for food.”

The woman sees it that way.

The serpent does not physically place the fruit in her hand.

She takes it.

The serpent does not feed her.

She eats.

This is why influence and agency cannot be treated as opposites here.

The woman’s agency has not disappeared.

It has been redirected through a changed evaluation of reality.

The serpent’s ideas have become consequential without becoming evidentially superior.

## 7. Where Was the Man?

The woman eats.

Then she gives fruit to her husband, and he eats.

Genesis tells us remarkably little about what happens between those actions.

It does not tell us where the man was during the serpent’s conversation.

It does not establish that he was standing beside the woman throughout it.

It does not establish that he was standing beside her when she ate.

Nor does Genesis tell us that he was somewhere else and that she later went looking for him.

We are not told what she said when she gave him the fruit.

We are not told what he understood about it.

We are not told what he thought.

We are not told why he ate.

That silence matters because it prevents us from turning either reconstruction into the explanation of what happened.

If the man was present, Genesis does not tell us that his silence caused the woman’s decision.

If he was absent, Genesis does not portray the woman as intellectually helpless without him.

Quite the opposite.

Her first recorded response to the serpent had demonstrated comprehension, memory, correction, and substantial knowledge of God’s instruction.

The narrative gives far more attention to the woman’s changing evaluation than to the man’s location.

And when it turns to him, it gives us the fact that matters for this movement:

she gave, and he ate.

Both humans have now crossed the boundary.

Their paths to that action should not be made identical merely because the final action is.

## 8. Their Eyes Opened. They Knew.

Now the competing description of reality begins encountering lived reality.

Here we should be careful with our language.

The serpent had said that eating would open their eyes.

Genesis says their eyes were opened.

The serpent had connected eating with new knowledge.

Genesis says they knew.

Those correspondences are real.

But calling them “predictions” can make the serpent sound as though it has demonstrated some extraordinary ability to foresee the future.

Genesis does not require us to give it that credit.

God had already established that crossing this boundary was consequential.

The serpent supplied a different description of what would follow the action.

Part of that description corresponds with what Genesis reports:

their eyes open,

and they know.

We should concede that fully.

If our portrait of the serpent requires us to pretend those correspondences do not exist, our portrait is too fragile.

But correctly naming a feature of an outcome is not the same thing as faithfully characterizing the outcome as lived.

The consequential question is not simply:

Did something the serpent said occur?

Something did.

The question becomes:

What is it actually like to possess what was presented as gain?

Genesis begins answering immediately.

“And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked.”

That sentence is strange.

Not because nakedness is strange.

Because they were already naked.

## 9. They Already Knew They Were Naked

Genesis had deliberately told us this before.

“The man and his wife were both naked, and were not ashamed.”

That was the before-state.

Their bodies were already unclothed.

Nothing suggests they were unable to see one another.

Nothing tells us they were unaware that they possessed bodies.

Nothing tells us nakedness suddenly came into existence after they ate.

Yet after eating:

their eyes are opened,

and they know they are naked.

Why “know”?

The word is especially striking because knowledge was the gain placed before them.

The serpent had said their eyes would open and they would know.

They eat.

Their eyes open.

They know.

Genesis does not stop to define this knowledge philosophically.

It shows us what the new knowing does to lived experience.

Before:

naked—and not ashamed.

After:

naked—they know—and they cover.

The material condition has not changed.

Their relationship to it has.

This does not require us to say that Genesis 3:7 has completely defined “the knowledge of good and evil.”

It has not.

Nor does verse 7 explicitly say:

“They were ashamed.”

Shame is strongly implicated because Genesis had deliberately told us they were previously naked and not ashamed.

But we do not need to reconstruct the complete emotional state.

The secure observation is already powerful:

nakedness has become something they experience as requiring concealment.

Nothing in the verse suggests they are responding to temperature.

No environmental danger appears.

Their bodies are not described as physically altered.

The sequence Genesis gives us is:

eyes opened → knew nakedness → made coverings.

Their changed knowing changes their relationship to an existing reality.

And this is where what happened with the tree happens again.

The tree did not change.

The woman’s relationship to the tree changed.

Their nakedness does not change.

Their relationship to nakedness changes.

Again:

same reality → different meaning → different action.

Genesis surrounds knowing with lived experience.

The first narrated manifestation of the promised knowledge is not merely that the humans can recite a new proposition.

They now inhabit an old reality differently.

Then they try to do something about it.

They sew fig leaves together.

They make coverings.

And once again, notice what they use.

Their intelligence.

Their agency.

Their constructive ability.

Vegetation from the world around them.

None of those things came from the serpent.

The serpent supplied another interpretation of God’s world.

It did not supply another world in which the humans could live.

Even now, as they respond to the condition entered through acting on that alternative interpretation, they remain dependent upon capacities and materials already belonging to the Creator’s provision.

Genesis 3:7 does not yet tell us whether their solution succeeds or fails.

It tells us that they perceive a problem and attempt a remedy.

At verse 7, that is enough.

## 10. Correctly Naming an Outcome Feature Is Not the Same as Faithfully Characterizing the Outcome

This is where a simple true-or-false test of the serpent's words becomes inadequate.

The serpent had said their eyes would be opened.

Their eyes were opened.

It had connected eating with knowledge.

They knew.

Those correspondences matter.

We should not diminish them because they make the serpent's words more complicated to evaluate. If the evidence gives the serpent correspondence at a particular point, then the evidence should receive its full weight.

But correspondence at one level does not settle the larger question.

The serpent did not merely say:

> Something will happen if you eat.

God had already said that eating was consequential.

The disagreement concerned what crossing the boundary meant.

The serpent placed opened eyes and knowledge inside a larger picture.

God knows something you do not.

The boundary stands between you and a changed condition.

Cross it, and your eyes will open.

Cross it, and you will know.

From the woman's side of the boundary, those features could be understood as gain.

Now the humans are on the other side.

Their eyes are open.

They know.

And Genesis immediately begins showing us what inhabiting that condition is actually like.

This is why correctly naming an outcome feature is not the same as faithfully characterizing the outcome.

Someone can correctly tell you that an action will produce a new experience without faithfully preparing you for what living inside that experience will mean.

The issue is not whether every possible consequence had to be disclosed.

That would demand too much.

The issue is whether the representation through which the action became desirable gave a faithful picture of the reality toward which it directed the hearer.

And Genesis has now given the reader something the woman did not possess before eating:

**the beginning of the lived result.**

The serpent's description can therefore be tested at more than one level.

At the level of individual outcome features:

**eyes opened — correspondence.**

**new knowing — correspondence.**

But at the level of the complete representation, another question remains:

> **Did the serpent faithfully characterize what entering this condition would mean for the humans?**

That question cannot be answered merely by pointing again to opened eyes.

Nor can it be answered merely by pointing to the word *knew*.

Those are real features of the outcome.

But Genesis does not leave them as abstractions.

It places them inside lived human experience.

And the first disclosed lived result is striking:

the humans possess the knowledge that had been presented as gain, and an old reality immediately becomes a new problem.

That does not prove that every word the serpent spoke was false.

It proves something more useful:

**propositional correspondence is not the same thing as representational faithfulness.**

A statement may correspond at the level of an individual proposition while the larger picture in which that proposition is placed still requires examination.

What was emphasized?

What was left unexplained?

What relationships were created among the facts?

What did those relationships make reasonable to anticipate?

And what was the reality like when it was finally lived?

Those questions matter because the serpent's influence did not depend upon fabricating every piece of reality.

As we have already seen, it could work with realities that were actually there.

There really was a boundary.

God really did know something the humans did not.

Their condition really would change.

Their eyes really would open.

They really would know.

The distortion did not require every piece to be false.

It required the pieces to be arranged into a picture capable of making boundary crossing look like gain.

Now reality has begun testing that picture.

And the first test does not require us to know everything that will follow.

The humans had already been naked.

Their bodies are not narrated as changing.

Yet after acquiring the knowledge associated with crossing the boundary, they experience their nakedness differently and begin trying to correct what they now perceive as a problem.

So the reader can concede everything that corresponds in the serpent's description without conceding that the serpent therefore represented reality faithfully.

In fact, refusing to concede the correspondence would make it harder to see what is so sophisticated about the distortion.

The serpent's alternative picture did not need to be completely detached from reality in order to redirect the humans.

It could contain real features.

It could correctly name something that would happen.

And those corresponding pieces could make the larger representation more persuasive.

The deeper test is whether the relationships among those pieces faithfully represented the reality the humans would inhabit.

Genesis 3:7 has begun answering that question.

Not completely.

But enough to prevent us from confusing a correct outcome feature with a faithful account of the outcome.

## 11. What Has Actually Changed About God?

Now we should return to the Person this study has been trying to know.

A great deal has changed since the serpent first spoke.

The woman's perception of the tree changed.

Her evaluation changed.

Her action changed.

The man ate.

Their eyes opened.

Their knowing changed.

Their relationship to nakedness changed.

Their behavior changed again.

But amid all of that change, another question deserves to be asked:

**What has actually changed about God?**

The serpent's picture of God has changed dramatically.

God's abundant provision has been made capable of looking incomplete.

God's boundary has been made capable of looking obstructive.

God's warning has been contradicted.

God's greater knowledge has been made capable of looking suspicious.

The humans' present condition has been made capable of looking deficient.

That is a radically different representation of God.

But a changed representation of God is not the same thing as changed evidence about God.

Before the serpent spoke, the humans were already living inside God's provision.

We have already seen the scale of that asymmetry.

The garden was there.

The food was there.

Their capacities were there.

Their agency was real.

Their relationship was already part of their experience.

God had communicated a boundary clearly enough for the woman herself to articulate it.

And the serpent supplied no comparable provision and no superior evidentiary history before asking its alternative picture to carry the weight of human action.

Nothing that happens through verse 7 retroactively erases that history.

The humans' changed experience of nakedness does not prove that their naked bodies had secretly been defective all along.

Genesis had already told us:

they were naked and not ashamed.

The later problem cannot become the original definition of the earlier condition.

Likewise, the fact that the woman came to see the tree differently does not establish that God's provision had actually been deficient before she changed her evaluation.

Her perception changed.

The provision did not first disappear.

And the fact that the serpent's ideas became influential does not establish that God's prior reliability had been evidentially overturned.

The picture of God changed before the evidence about God did.

That distinction is central.

### The Boundary From the Other Side

The prohibition itself can now be examined from a position the humans did not possess before eating.

Before the crossing, the serpent's representation made the boundary capable of looking like something standing between the humans and desirable advancement.

God knows.

You do not.

Cross the boundary, and your eyes will open.

Cross it, and you will know.

Within that picture, the prohibition can look like restriction imposed against human gain.

But after eating, the humans enter an experiential condition Genesis had not previously disclosed in them.

Their eyes open.

They know.

Their relationship to their own nakedness changes.

They begin trying to address a condition they did not previously experience as requiring correction.

Whatever else “knowledge of good and evil” will prove to encompass, one thing is now visible:

**the prohibition had stood between the humans and this experience.**

That is an observable effect.

And that makes a protective purpose of God in giving the prohibition increasingly plausible.

But the effect is clearer than the purpose.

Genesis has shown us what the boundary stood between.

It has not yet given us permission to say that we know every reason God established it.

There may be dimensions of purpose still undisclosed.

But uncertainty about complete purpose should not make us pretend the observable effect is uncertain too.

Before crossing, this condition had not been disclosed in the humans.

After crossing, it is.

The boundary had stood between them and a way of knowing they now experience from the inside.

That does not complete our understanding of God.

It does, however, give the reader reason to reconsider the serpent's portrayal of the prohibition as an obstacle to human good.

The same boundary that could be made to look like deprivation from one side can now be seen, from the other side, as having stood between the humans and a condition they had never before needed to manage.

That is not yet the same as knowing God's complete purpose.

But it is more evidence than the humans possessed when the serpent first made the boundary look suspicious.

## 12. And Where Was God?

There is another question that naturally presses upon the scene.

Where was God while all of this was happening?

He does not narratively interrupt the serpent's opening question.

He does not interrupt its contradiction.

He does not enter the conversation when the serpent begins making claims about what God knows.

He does not stop the woman's evaluation from changing.

He does not prevent her from taking the fruit.

He does not stop the man from eating.

That silence can seem strange if we encounter it as though Genesis has never shown God allowing consequential human action to proceed without continual intervention.

But it has.

Before any misuse of human agency had been narrated, God brought the animals to the man.

The man encountered them.

And he named them.

Genesis attributes those naming decisions to the man.

God did not dictate each name.

He did not interrupt every judgment.

He remained Creator while allowing human agency to have consequential standing within the world he had made.

That matters because it gives us a positive-priority baseline for human agency and divine non-intervention.

**Non-intervention during consequential human agency existed before human agency was misused.**

So God's silence in Genesis 3 should not automatically be interpreted through the negative context in which we now encounter it.

The circumstances are radically different.

Naming animals and crossing a divine boundary are not morally equivalent actions.

But the relational point is narrower:

God's sovereignty had already been shown to coexist with meaningful human volition without continual narrated interruption.

That means God's present silence is not, by itself, evidence that God has suddenly become absent.

Nor does it establish that he approves of what is happening.

Nor does it establish that he commissioned the serpent.

Nor can we simply turn the silence into a complete theory of free will and say:

> God remains silent because freedom requires it.

Genesis has not told us that.

The purpose of the silence remains undisclosed.

But its existence is not without precedent.

And calling it evidence of indifference would require us to forget almost everything Genesis has already shown us about God's involvement.

Creation is involvement.

Provision is involvement.

Blessing is involvement.

Relationship is involvement.

Communication is involvement.

Giving humans meaningful responsibility is involvement.

The prohibition is involvement.

The warning is involvement.

God does not first become involved after something goes wrong.

He has been involved from the beginning.

The humans therefore do not meet the serpent's competing representation abandoned to an evidentiary vacuum.

They meet it with a world already provided.

With capacities already given.

With a relationship already established.

With a boundary already communicated.

With a consequence already disclosed.

And with genuine agency already demonstrated.

God does not narratively interrupt the decision.

But neither has he left the humans with nothing by which to make it.

## 13. The Reality Behind the Picture

Genesis 3:1–7 begins with a question about what God said.

By verse 7, the humans experience their own bodies differently.

That is an enormous distance.

Yet Genesis lets us follow the movement.

Nothing in the garden has changed when the serpent begins speaking.

What changes first is the representation.

The serpent focuses attention on the boundary.

The woman initially corrects it.

The serpent intensifies the challenge.

It contradicts the consequence.

It claims knowledge.

It takes realities God has already disclosed and rearranges the relationships among them.

Provision can now contain deprivation.

Boundary can become obstruction.

Warning can become misinformation.

Difference in knowledge can become concealed advantage.

Present human condition can become deficiency.

Crossing can become gain.

The serpent supplies no alternative world.

It supplies no comparable history of provision.

It supplies no evidence that makes its alternative account more reasonable than the reality the humans already inhabit.

**And yet it works.**

Not by eliminating human agency.

By becoming influential within it.

The woman begins evaluating the same tree differently.

The tree has not changed.

Its meaning for her has.

She acts.

The man eats.

Their eyes open.

They know.

Then another unchanged reality is experienced differently.

Their nakedness has not newly appeared.

Their bodies are not narrated as changing.

Yet nakedness now becomes something they seek to conceal.

The tree changed without changing.

Then nakedness changed without changing.

Between those two moments stands the extraordinary power of a competing picture of God to become a way of seeing everything else.

This is why Genesis 3:1–7 is more than a story about a broken rule.

It is showing us how beliefs about God can become relationships with reality.

What we believe about God can affect what looks abundant and what looks scarce.

What looks protective and what looks restrictive.

What looks sufficient and what looks deficient.

What looks like wisdom and what looks like deprivation.

And because human agency is genuine, those interpretations can become actions.

Actions can become experiences.

Experiences can then reveal dimensions of the representation that were difficult to see from the other side of the action.

That is exactly what has begun to happen by verse 7.

The serpent said their eyes would open.

They did.

It connected eating with knowing.

They knew.

Those correspondences deserve to stand.

But Genesis does not stop with them.

It lets us see the humans beginning to live inside what those words meant.

And the lived result raises a larger question than whether the serpent correctly named an outcome feature.

It asks whether the picture through which the humans were induced to cross the boundary faithfully characterized the reality they would inhabit.

Alongside that question stands another.

Had God's words described reality more faithfully than the alternative picture that made crossing his boundary look like gain?

At verse 7, we do not yet possess every answer.

God's warning about death has not been exhaustively explained.

The complete meaning of the knowledge of good and evil has not been exhausted.

God's complete purpose in giving the prohibition has not been disclosed.

The humans' coverings have not been evaluated as successful or unsuccessful.

There are questions Genesis 3:7 simply does not answer.

We should let them remain questions.

But unanswered questions do not erase the evidence we do possess.

God's provision preceded the competing representation.

The humans were already living inside it.

The serpent supplied no comparable provision.

It supplied no evidentiary basis that made its account more reasonable.

The woman initially corrected its distortion.

Only later did the prohibited alternative become attractive.

Her agency remained real as her evaluation changed.

The humans crossed the boundary.

Their eyes opened.

They knew.

And the first disclosed lived manifestation of that knowing changed their relationship to a reality that had not changed.

By the end of verse 7, reality has begun adjudicating the competing pictures.

Not completely.

But genuinely.

And perhaps that is where this study should leave us.

Not with a demand that we decide every theological question Genesis will eventually raise.

Not with a caricature in which God says only true things and the serpent says only false things.

Not with a woman reduced to gullibility.

Not with a man whose undisclosed psychology we invent.

Not with a boundary whose complete purpose we pretend to know.

But with the evidence Genesis has actually placed before us.

One representation came from the Creator whose provision the humans already inhabited.

The other came from a creature that supplied neither an alternative world nor superior evidence, but offered another way of interpreting the world already given.

That alternative interpretation became influential.

The humans acted inside it.

And then they began to know what living inside its consequences was like.

The final question Genesis 3:1–7 leaves before the reader is therefore not merely:

> **Who said something technically correct?**

It is:

> **Which representation of God more faithfully prepared the humans for the reality they actually entered?**

By verse 7, the evidence has begun to answer.

And that is where we should let the evidence stop.
