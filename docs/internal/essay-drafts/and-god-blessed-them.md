# **And God Blessed Them**

### *Genesis 1:20–25 and the first blessing of life*

There is something almost understated about the arrival of animal life in Genesis.

“Let the waters swarm with swarms of living creatures,” God says, “and let birds fly above the earth.”

And then they do.

That is nearly all the drama Genesis gives it.

By this point in the story, we have watched light appear, waters divide, dry land emerge, vegetation grow, and lights take their places in the heavens. But living creatures introduce something different. These creatures move. They reproduce. They carry within themselves the capacity for generations that do not yet exist.

A modern reader knows enough about living organisms to feel the weight hidden inside these few sentences. Reproduction involves staggering biological complexity. What Genesis compresses into “be fruitful and multiply” encompasses realities its first readers could never have described as we can.

But Genesis does not stop to explain any of that.

There is no indication that life presents God with a new level of difficulty. No struggle. No experiment. No discovery of how to make living things work.

The narrative remains in the same remarkable register:

God speaks.

Life appears.

Perhaps the understatement itself is worth noticing.

## What Genesis chooses not to explain

There are countless questions we could ask.

How did life begin?

What processes were involved?

What exactly does Genesis mean by creatures being made “according to their kinds”?

Where do creatures that cross the categories fit—animals that live between land and water, for example?

How does reproduction work?

Genesis does not answer these questions.

That does not make them bad questions.

It simply means they are not the questions this passage chooses to answer.

Instead, amid everything Genesis could have told us about the first great abundance of animal life, it tells us something else:

> **God blessed them.**

That is new.

God has spoken.

God has separated.

God has named.

God has seen.

God has called things good.

Now, for the first time in the narrative, **God blesses**.

That may tell us more about what Genesis wants us to notice than an explanation of biology would have.

## A future inside the blessing

The blessing is not left completely undefined.

God says:

> “Be fruitful and multiply and fill the waters in the seas, and let birds multiply on the earth.”

God does not merely create living creatures and leave us with the fact that they exist.

He directs the blessing toward their future.

**Be fruitful.**

**Multiply.**

**Fill.**

Life is given the capacity to extend beyond the creatures God has just made.

There will be another generation.

And another.

The seas are to fill.

Birds are to multiply across the earth.

This is different from the inanimate creation we have encountered. Light is not told to produce more light. The expanse is not blessed to reproduce. The seas are not commanded to make additional seas.

But living creatures receive the ability to produce life beyond themselves, and Genesis places that capacity inside God's blessing.

We do not need to know how reproduction works to recognize what the passage has made clear:

**God positively wills a future for this life.**

Not necessarily an endless future—the passage does not say that.

But certainly a future beyond the first generation.

## Comprehension isn't what makes it effective

Then something curious appears.

Genesis tells us that God blessed the creatures.

It does not tell us that they understood.

We are not shown fish recognizing that they have been blessed.

Birds do not answer God.

There is no agreement.

No confession.

No promise.

No indication that these creatures even comprehend what has just been given to them.

Yet Genesis does not appear troubled by that.

The blessing is still a blessing.

That means we should be careful about importing something into blessing that its first appearance does not require.

> **Comprehension isn't what makes it effective.**

That does not tell us everything blessing means.

It tells us something blessing apparently does **not** require.

At least here, the reality of God's blessing is not made dependent upon demonstrated awareness in the recipient.

God can act beneficially toward a creature before that creature has shown understanding what God has done.

## Before anything is earned

There is another absence worth noticing.

Nothing precedes this blessing that could be described as achievement.

The creatures have not proved themselves.

They have not obeyed a command and then received blessing as a reward.

They have not negotiated terms.

They have not entered a contract.

They have not promised God anything.

They have only just come into existence.

And God blesses them.

Later Scripture may give us covenants, conditions, commands, consequences, and promises. Those later relationships should be allowed to speak when we reach them.

But they should not be allowed to rewrite blessing's first appearance.

Here, blessing does not begin as payment for performance.

It begins with God.

## What about the land animals?

That makes what happens next especially interesting.

When Genesis turns to the land animals, the explicit blessing formula is not repeated.

God says that the earth should bring forth living creatures according to their kinds. The animals appear. God makes them according to their kinds.

And:

> God sees that it is good.

Why isn't the blessing repeated?

The passage does not tell us.

It would be easy to make the silence answer the question for us.

Perhaps God cared more about the fish and birds.

Perhaps the land animals were somehow excluded from his positive interest.

But Genesis has not said either of those things.

What it *has* told us is that God makes these creatures, differentiates them, attends to the result, and calls it good.

So the absence of a narrated blessing cannot simply become evidence for the absence of divine care.

Sometimes the responsible conclusion is smaller:

> **The blessing isn't repeated, and we do not know why.**

That does not weaken what we already know.

It simply leaves the question where Genesis leaves it.

## The earth participates, but God still makes

There is another curious feature in this movement.

God says:

> “Let the earth bring forth living creatures...”

The earth participates.

We have seen something like this before with vegetation. God does not narrate created reality as completely passive.

Yet only a few words later Genesis says that **God made** the land animals.

The narrative seems perfectly comfortable holding both statements together.

The earth brings forth.

God makes.

Genesis does not stop to explain how those descriptions relate.

Nor does it appear to regard them as competitors.

That gives us another place where explanation is incomplete while the picture remains surprisingly clear.

Created participation does not make God disappear.

And divine agency does not require creation to do nothing.

How those belong together remains open.

But Genesis gives us both.

## Before life gives anything back

By the end of these verses, something important has happened in our developing portrait of God.

Animal life has arrived.

Before Genesis records these creatures doing anything toward God, God has already acted toward them.

He creates them.

He differentiates them.

He attends to what has resulted.

He calls it good.

And, for the creatures of the waters and the air, he blesses them toward abundance and a future.

There is still no reciprocal relationship described.

No creature praises him.

No creature thanks him.

No creature promises loyalty.

No creature demonstrates that it even understands what has happened.

That means “intimacy” may still be too strong a word for what we have seen. Intimacy normally suggests something mutual.

But **beneficence** is not too strong.

God's beneficial action toward life precedes demonstrated reciprocation from life.

And that is worth remembering.

Genesis has still left us with enormous questions.

We do not know how life was produced.

We do not know precisely what a “kind” is.

We do not know how blessing operates.

We do not know why the blessing is explicitly narrated for some creatures and not repeated for the land animals.

But perhaps those unanswered questions need not become crises.

Because the passage has not left everything uncertain.

Among everything it could have told us about the arrival of animal life, Genesis makes sure we see this:

> **God created life.**
>
> **God saw it.**
>
> **God called it good.**
>
> **And God blessed them.**

The explanation is incomplete.

The Person is becoming less so.
