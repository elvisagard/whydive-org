# **When the Making Was Finished**

### *Genesis 1:26–2:3*

Something changes when Genesis reaches humanity.

Until now, God has spoken light, sky, land, vegetation, lights, fish, birds, and land animals into the developing creation. Again and again, God speaks, something happens, and God sees.

Then the language changes.

> “Let us make humankind in our image, according to our likeness...”

For the first time, God speaks of **“us.”**

For the first time, a creature is described in relation to **God's own image and likeness**.

And for the first time, the narrative moves toward a creature God will directly address.

It feels like a climax.

It is.

But surprisingly, it is not the end.

## “Let us make”

Genesis does not explain the “us.”

That is worth admitting before trying to solve it.

Earlier, the narrative mentioned the Spirit of God hovering over the waters. A reader may naturally wonder whether that earlier disclosure has something to do with the plural language here. But Genesis does not identify the one or ones being addressed.

What it does make difficult to miss is the plurality itself:

**Let us make.**

**Our image.**

**Our likeness.**

Then, almost immediately, the language returns to singular attribution:

> “So God created humankind in his image,
> in the image of God he created them...”

Whatever we eventually conclude about the “us,” Genesis has given us more information about God than we possessed before—and has done so without explaining all of it.

That has happened repeatedly in this story.

The unexplained is not necessarily the undisclosed.

Sometimes Genesis tells us something clearly enough to recognize it while leaving us unable to explain it completely.

## Continuity—and Something New

Human beings arrive with striking continuity with the life that came before them.

Animals were created.

Humans are created.

Animals were blessed.

Humans are blessed.

Animals reproduce.

Humans are told to be fruitful and multiply.

Animals inhabit the created world.

Humans inhabit that same world.

And yet Genesis does not introduce humanity merely as another creature “according to its kind.”

Instead:

> **“in our image, according to our likeness.”**

Genesis does not stop to explain what image and likeness mean.

But it is difficult to miss the distinction.

The most familiar creature to the human reader is introduced with some of the most mysterious language in the chapter.

Again, the familiar and unexplained sit beside each other without apology.

Humanity belongs to the creation that came before it.

And humanity receives a treatment nothing before it received.

## Authority before conflict

God also says:

> “Let them have dominion...”

Dominion can be an uncomfortable word.

We know what domination can look like. Human history gives us more than enough examples of power becoming oppression.

But none of that history exists inside this story yet.

There is no war.

No rebellion.

No oppression.

No narrated evil.

No human hierarchy.

The creatures over which humanity receives dominion belong to a world God has been calling good.

Whatever dominion will eventually mean, its first appearance is not inside a conflict narrative.

**Authority enters the story before conflict does.**

And the stated domain is notable.

Genesis does not say that some humans are to have dominion over other humans. Humanity receives dominion over the living creation that preceded it.

That does not tell us everything dominion means.

But it gives us a starting point that later examples of abusive power should not be allowed to erase.

## “I have given you”

Then God speaks directly to humanity.

That alone is new.

The animals received blessing without Genesis showing us whether they understood what had happened. Now the narrative explicitly gives us a recipient capable of being addressed:

> “God said to them...”

And then:

> **“Behold, I have given you...”**

Genesis could simply have told us that food existed.

Instead God identifies himself as the giver.

The difference is small in words and enormous in relationship.

There are plants.

There are fruit-bearing trees.

But humanity is not merely left to discover that these things are available.

God says:

> **I have given you.**

Provision has become **disclosed provision**.

Humans are allowed to know not only that something has been provided, but **who gave it**.

The chapter still does not explain how most of creation works.

It has left unanswered questions about light, waters, stars, life, reproduction, kinds, image, likeness, and now dominion.

But it does not leave the identity of the giver uncertain.

## Before humanity says anything back

And then something remarkable happens.

Nothing.

At least, nothing from humanity.

Humans do not thank God.

They do not worship.

They do not promise obedience.

They do not negotiate their responsibilities.

They do not ask for food.

They do not say anything at all.

That does not mean they were incapable of response.

It means **Genesis does not make their response the subject of this movement**.

Before Genesis tells us anything humanity will do with God, it gives us a history of what God does toward humanity.

He intends them.

He creates them.

He describes them relative to himself.

He blesses them.

He gives them responsibility.

He provides for them.

And he tells them about the provision.

The initiative remains with God.

## Very good

Then God looks.

He has done this before.

Light was good.

Land and sea were good.

Vegetation was good.

The lights were good.

Animate life was good.

Land animals were good.

But now the field of vision expands:

> **“God saw everything that he had made...”**

Everything.

And:

> **“it was very good.”**

This is not merely God's appraisal of humanity.

It is his appraisal of the whole.

Humanity may occupy a climactic position among the physical creatures, but Genesis does not allow humanity to swallow the value of everything that came before.

God looks at **all that he has made**.

Very good.

And still, evil has not been introduced as the thing against which goodness must be measured.

Good came first.

God has been recognizing it all along.

## It could have ended here

This would make a perfectly understandable ending.

Everything has been created.

Humanity has arrived.

God has addressed humanity.

Provision has been disclosed.

God surveys the whole.

Very good.

End.

But Genesis continues.

And that continuation changes how we understand the culmination of the story.

> “Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all their host.”

The narrative now tells us explicitly that the work is complete.

God has not merely stopped being described as creating.

**The work is finished.**

That distinction matters.

## God finished

Genesis does not tell us that God stopped because creating had exhausted him.

It does not tell us that he encountered something he could not overcome.

It does not tell us that there was nothing else God could possibly have created.

There is no rival.

No resistance.

No shortage.

No interruption.

The explanation we are given is much simpler:

**the work was finished.**

And God ceased from the work he had been doing.

God does not stop because something stops him.

**God stops because the work is finished.**

That adds something new to the portrait.

For six days, we have watched God initiate and act.

Now we see his relationship to completion.

God's activity is not endless activity merely for activity's sake.

The work reaches an endpoint.

And Genesis makes sure we know it.

## When God stopped creating

But God does not disappear.

This is important.

God ceases from his creative work and then immediately continues acting.

He blesses.

He sanctifies.

So cessation from creating cannot mean cessation of all divine activity.

Nor does finishing the physical creation mean the end of God's interest in what has happened.

Something else follows.

And whatever that something is, Genesis considers it important enough that the creation story does not end until we have seen it.

## A blessing that cannot mean reproduction

Earlier, God blessed the creatures of the sea and sky:

> Be fruitful. Multiply. Fill.

Then humanity received reproductive blessing as well.

That could tempt us to conclude that blessing is essentially reproductive empowerment.

The seventh day prevents us from doing that.

> **“God blessed the seventh day...”**

A day does not reproduce.

Whatever blessing means, reproduction cannot exhaust it.

And Genesis does not help us much further.

It tells us unmistakably **what God did**.

It does not tell us what blessing a day accomplishes.

That may be frustrating if we expect every important statement to arrive with its explanation.

But Genesis has been doing this from the beginning.

We can know:

> **God blessed the seventh day**

more confidently than we know:

> **what the blessing did.**

The explanation can remain open without making the event uncertain.

## Holy before anything went wrong

Then Genesis gives us another first:

> **God sanctified the seventh day.**

The first holiness in the biblical story appears before anything has gone wrong.

There is no sin.

No impurity.

No sacrifice.

No priesthood.

No salvation from a broken condition.

No conflict.

Yet something is holy.

That matters.

It means holiness does not first enter Genesis as a solution to evil.

It exists inside a creation God has just called **very good**.

Just as good did not require evil in order to be good, holiness does not require impurity in order to appear.

Later Scripture may place holiness beside sin, impurity, worship, priesthood, sacrifice, and salvation.

Those later relationships will deserve to be studied when they arrive.

But they should not be allowed to rewrite the first appearance backward.

Here, holiness is something God establishes **before there is anything to repair**.

## Holy time

And what does God sanctify?

Not another creature.

Not a mountain.

Not an altar.

Not a temple.

Not a human being.

A day.

A portion of time.

The physical creation is already complete.

Nothing else is brought into existence.

Yet God's culminating activity establishes a distinction within time itself.

He blesses the seventh day.

He makes it holy.

We should be careful not to make that say more than Genesis says.

The passage does not yet command humans to rest.

It does not tell them to worship on this day.

It does not say that they observed it.

It does not explain what the blessing accomplishes.

It does not tell us that everything preceding the seventh day was created *for* the seventh day.

But it does tell us where the literary movement arrives:

**completed creation.**

**divine cessation.**

**blessed time.**

**holy time.**

Humanity may be the climactic physical creature.

But the story does not culminate with another physical thing.

## The ending Genesis does not give us

There is one final surprise.

Six times Genesis has trained us to expect a familiar ending:

> there was evening, and there was morning...

First day.

Second day.

Third.

Fourth.

Fifth.

Sixth.

The pattern becomes so familiar that it is easy to remember the seventh day ending the same way.

It does not.

Genesis never gives the seventh day the evening-and-morning formula used for the other six.

What does that mean?

The text does not tell us.

And perhaps that is where we should stop.

The omission is real.

Its explanation remains open.

There is no need to turn it into an endless day, a metaphorical continuation, or a theory of eternity simply because we have discovered the pattern break.

Sometimes the text gives us a distinction more clearly than it gives us the explanation for the distinction.

This is one of those times.

## What God does when the making is finished

We began this creation movement watching God work.

God spoke.

God separated.

God named.

God made.

God created.

God saw.

God evaluated.

God blessed.

God gave.

And Genesis does not leave us wondering whether the creative work simply faded from view.

It tells us when the work was finished.

Then it lets us watch what God does next.

He ceases.

He blesses.

He makes time holy.

Humans still have not asked for any of this.

They have not negotiated it.

They have not explained it.

They have not even spoken.

The initiative remains where it has been from the beginning.

With God.

And perhaps that is why the final three verses matter so much.

The story of creation does not end merely because there is nothing more being made.

**It ends after we are shown what the Creator does when his making is finished.**
